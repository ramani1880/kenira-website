Why your product photos look cheap (it's the light, not the camera)

Cheap-looking product photos are almost always a lighting problem. What I fix first, using a window, one light color and a white card.

When a client sends me product photos and asks why they look cheap, I know the answer before I open the files. It's the light. It's almost never the camera.

A new phone takes a sharp picture. Sharp isn't the problem. The problem is hard, mixed, badly placed light, and no camera fixes that after the fact.

Small light makes small-looking products

The size of the light compared to the product decides almost everything. A bare bulb or the ceiling light in your office is a tiny source. Tiny sources make hard shadows with sharp edges and harsh hot spots on anything shiny. That look reads as "taken in a back room," because that's usually where it was taken.

A big, soft source does the opposite. Shadows fade out gently, edges look smooth, and the product looks like it belongs in a catalog. The cheapest big soft light I know is a window with no direct sun coming through it. Put the product beside the window, not in front of it, so the light falls across it and shows the shape.

Mixed color is the silent killer

Daylight from the window, warm bulbs overhead, a cool screen glowing nearby. Your eye adjusts to all of it. The camera doesn't. You get a product that's blue on one side and orange on the other, and no amount of editing makes white packaging look white again.

Turn off every light you aren't using on purpose. One color of light, every time. It takes ten seconds and fixes more photos than any filter.

Fill the shadow, don't kill it

Once the light comes from one side, the other side goes dark. People panic and add a second lamp, and now two shadows fight each other. I'd rather bounce. A sheet of white foam board on the shadow side throws some of the window light back in. Move it closer for a brighter fill, further away for more mood. You keep one clean shadow, and that shadow is what makes a product look solid and real.

Shiny things need a different plan

Glass, metal, glossy boxes: anything reflective shows you exactly what's around it. That includes your ceiling, your shirt and the phone in your hand. With shiny products I'm not really lighting the product. I'm lighting what it reflects. A large white card placed where the reflection lands turns an ugly bright blob into a clean highlight along the edge.

Where my time actually goes

On a product shoot, the camera settings take me a few minutes. The light takes most of the day. I move a panel a few inches, check the shot, move it again. It's slow on purpose, because every product has its own shape and finish, and the light has to fit it.

If your photos look cheap, don't buy a new camera first. Find the biggest soft light you have, turn off everything else, and add one white card. Then look again. If you want them to look like the brand you're actually building, that's the part I do.
