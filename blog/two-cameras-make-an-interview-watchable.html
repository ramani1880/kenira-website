One camera films the interview. Two cameras make it watchable.

A locked-off single shot is honest and boring. The second angle is not decoration. It is the whole reason people watch to the end.

Watch a single-camera interview for two minutes and you will feel it. The shot never changes, the person talks, and your eyes start looking for the exit. It does not matter how good the answer is. One unchanging frame reads as a security camera, not a story.

Add a second camera and the same interview turns into something people finish. A wide shot sets the room, a tight shot holds the face, and the cut between them does quiet work. It hides the pause where they searched for a word. It lets a reaction land. It gives the viewer a reason to stay for the next sentence.

What the second camera actually buys

It buys the edit. With one angle, every cut is a jump. The head snaps, the background shifts, and the viewer notices the edit instead of the point. With two angles, I cut on a thought, not on a mistake. The wide shot covers the breath, the tight shot carries the line that matters, and the whole thing feels like a conversation instead of a deposition.

It also buys insurance. If the tight camera catches a blink or a truck rolls past outside, the wide shot saves the take. I have salvaged interviews in a Plymouth Meeting office this way. One camera means one point of failure. Two means a backup that also looks better.

The honest cost

A second camera is not free, and I will not pretend it is. It is a second body, lens, card, and batteries. Locked off on a tripod, the cost is mostly gear and setup time. With an operator, it is a second person for the day. Either way the edit takes longer, because two angles mean twice the footage to sort and sync. On a 90-second social clip, that cost is hard to justify. On a three-minute founder story that lives on a homepage for two years, it is cheap.

How I set it up

The wide camera sits straight on, at eye level, and it does not move. That is the anchor. The tight camera sits about 30 degrees to one side, closer in, on the face and hands. Both shoot the same frame rate and the same color settings, or the cut will flash every time I switch. I clap once on camera so the two files sync in seconds, and I check both cards before anyone leaves the room. A second camera you forgot to press record on is just an expensive tripod.

When one camera is enough

Not every job needs two. A single answer for social media, a quick update from the owner, a clip under a minute: one good angle, well lit, will do it. I tell clients this plainly, because selling a second camera on a 45-second clip is selling gear, not value. But the moment the video runs past two minutes, or the moment it has to carry a real story, one angle starts working against you. Viewers do not leave because the content got boring. They leave because the picture stopped changing.

So my rule is simple. Short and simple, one camera. Anything meant to be watched all the way through, two. The second camera is not a luxury upgrade. It is the difference between footage that exists and a video people actually finish.
